Today those placeholder pages grow up. Alex, what makes a page real?
Alex: A complete document with the same skeleton as every other page on the site. Each page starts with the doctype, declares the language as English, sets the character encoding, and carries its own unique title, the text that appears on the browser tab. Then the body follows one skeleton everywhere: a header with the site name, a nav with the links, a main with the page's content, and a footer with the small print. Same order, same landmarks, every page.
Jordan: Why does the skeleton have to match everywhere? My pages will have different content anyway.
Alex: Because visitors learn your site once. The nav sits in the same place on every page, so nobody hunts for it. And those landmarks, header, nav, main, footer, are not just boxes: they are meaning. A screen reader can jump straight to the main content, skipping the nav it has already heard. A search engine knows the footer is not the article. When every page shares the skeleton, assistive technology can navigate your whole site on autopilot.
Jordan: And the headings? I know there is a rule about exactly one h one.
Alex: Exactly one h one per page, and a heading outline with no skipped levels: h one, then h two, then h three, never h one straight to h three. Think of the h one as the page's topic, the single answer to the question, what is this page about. The h twos are its chapters, the h threes are sections within a chapter. Skip a level and it reads like a book that jumps from chapter one to chapter three. The course checker flags skipped levels, and the project rubric scores the outline, so this is not decoration, it is structure.
Jordan: Let me make that concrete. Say I picked the personal portfolio theme and I am building the contact page.
Alex: The h one is Contact, the page's topic, and it appears once. Under it, an h two for Send me a message and another h two for Find me online. If the message section needs subsections, those become h threes. One topic, chapters beneath it, sections beneath those. A visitor skimming just the headings should get the whole page.
Jordan: Second thread: the smaller elements that carry meaning. Lists, links, images. What is the rule?
Alex: Use a list wherever the content really is a list. Nav links are a list. A recipe's ingredients are a list. Steps, features, frequently asked questions, all lists. If you would put bullets or numbers on it on paper, it is a list in H T M L. And link the pages together with relative links, plain file names like about dot html, never paths from your own computer.
Jordan: I have definitely seen beginners link to C colon backslash something. Why does that break?
Alex: Because that path exists only on their machine. The moment the site is published, the server has no C drive with their files, and every link dies. A relative link travels with the site: about dot html means the file sitting next to this one, wherever the site happens to live. The checker flags Windows paths and file colon slash slash paths for exactly this reason. Relative links are portable links.
Jordan: And images? Everybody knows the alt attribute exists, and half the web leaves it empty.
Alex: Meaningful alt text describes the image for someone who cannot see it. A photo of your coffee shop gets alt text like, barista pouring latte art into a cup on the wooden counter, not the word image, and not a sentence about how pretty it is. Decorative images, the ones that add no information, get an empty alt, which tells a screen reader to skip them politely. And at least one image should live inside a figure with a caption: the caption is visible to everyone and says what the image shows, like a screenshot of a project with a caption saying what you built.
Jordan: Third thread: real content, the validator, and that magic trick from the cold open. Walk me through the order.
Alex: First, replace the placeholder text with real writing on the home page and at least one inner page. The rubric scores real, well-written content, and placeholder paragraphs are easy points to lose. Write like a human: the coffee shop's actual story, the club's actual meeting time. Second, run every page through the W 3 C validator and fix every error. The validator is a strict proofreader: it catches unclosed tags, misplaced elements, and typos in attribute names, the mistakes your eyes slide right past.
Jordan: Give me a concrete validator moment, so I know what it feels like.
Alex: You paste in your menu page and the validator reports an unclosed list item three quarters of the way down. You open the file, and there it is: a list item you opened and never closed, which swallowed the next two items into itself. One closing tag, revalidate, green. That is the whole workflow, and the milestone is not done until every page passes with no errors.
Jordan: And then the no stylesheet test.
Alex: The best test in the milestone, and it takes ten seconds. Remove the stylesheet link, reload, and read. The nav should read as a plain list of links. Headings should step down in order. The figure caption should sit with its image. If the page still makes sense top to bottom, your H T M L is doing its job and C S S only has to decorate. If the page is a confusing pile, the problem is in the markup, and no amount of styling will fix meaning that is not there.
Jordan: I want the traps again. What catches people in milestone two?
