Jordan: What does a proper table need, structurally? Beginners usually just make rows and cells.
Alex: Four structural pieces. A caption, the table's title living inside the table, so anyone jumping straight to it knows what the numbers are about. A head grouping the header row and a body grouping the data rows, so styles can treat each part differently. And header cells with the scope attribute saying what each header describes: scope column for headers labeling the column below them, scope row for headers labeling the row beside them, like the days down the side of a timetable.
Jordan: Why does scope matter? The bold centered look is just a browser default anyway.
Alex: Because meaning, not looks, is the point. A screen reader meeting a timetable cell needs to announce it as seven o'clock, Monday, spin class, not a lonely spin class. The scope attribute is what makes that possible: it tells the browser which header describes which cell. Get the structure right and the table is readable by everyone, including visitors who never see it.

Jordan: Second thread: the form, where the visitor talks back. What is the first rule?
Alex: Every control gets a visible label, connected in the code with the label's for attribute matching the control's id. And tattoo this next to it: a placeholder is not a label. Placeholder text vanishes the moment the visitor starts typing, it is often too pale to read, and screen readers may skip it entirely. The label stays visible, always. Clicking a label even focuses its control, which turns a tiny checkbox into a big comfortable target.
Jordan: Grouping next. Fieldsets and legends sound old-fashioned.
Alex: They are old and they are essential. A fieldset groups related controls, and its legend is the group's question. Every radio button group needs one, because without it a screen reader hears three orphaned buttons, small, medium, large, with no idea what they choose. With it, the visitor hears one question, pizza size, with three options. The chapter's form is full of these natural groups: contact details together, event details together, choices together.
Jordan: Then the craft of picking the right control for each answer.
Alex: Choose the type that fits the data. Email for email addresses, telephone for phone numbers, number for guest counts, date for event dates, a dropdown list for packages, a multi-line box for dietary notes. Two payoffs, and the first is in your pocket: on a phone, the email type summons a keyboard with the at sign right there, and the number type summons a number pad. The second is free checking: the browser can refuse a nonsense email address or an empty required field before anything is sent. And every control needs a name, because when the form submits, the browser collects one answer from each named control, and a control with no name sends nothing at all.
Jordan: The concrete example here is the catering request form, right?
