Web Media

Photos, logos, icons, sound, video and color carry most of what a page communicates, and most of its weight. In this chapter you will look inside an image down to the single pixel, learn why the same picture can weigh 6 MB or 60 KB, pick the right format for every job, put audio and video on a page that everyone can use, and choose colors that work together and stay readable.

Representing Digital Images

Everything a computer stores is a number, and a picture is no exception. To save an image, a program has to turn what you see into numbers, and to show it, the browser has to turn those numbers back into light on your screen. There are two very different ways to do that, and every image format on the web uses one of them:

  • Raster images (also called bitmaps) store a grid of tiny colored squares called pixels. Photos, screenshots and scans are raster images. JPEG, PNG, GIF, WebP and AVIF are raster formats.
  • Vector images store drawing instructions instead: "a circle here, this big, filled with this color; a line from here to there". Logos, icons and illustrations are often vectors. SVG is the vector format of the web.

Most of this chapter is about raster images, because that is what photos are and where most of a page's weight comes from. You will meet vectors again at the end of this section.

Pixels: a picture made of numbers

A pixel (short for "picture element") is the smallest dot of color in a raster image. The image is a grid of them: a photo that is 1920 × 1080 has 1,920 pixels in each row and 1,080 rows, which is 2,073,600 pixels in all. The two numbers are the image's dimensions, always written width first.

Each pixel stores exactly one color, written as numbers. Zoom in far enough on any photo and the smooth picture falls apart into squares. The scene below is tiny on purpose: only 48 pixels wide and 32 tall. Magnify it, then point at a pixel (or click the picture and use the arrow keys) to read the numbers it actually stores. Look closely where the sun meets the sky: those edge pixels hold in-between colors. That blending, called anti-aliasing, is how a grid of squares fakes a smooth curve.

    Color channels: mixing red, green and blue light

    Look at a screen through a magnifying glass and you will see that every pixel is really three tiny lights: one red, one green, one blue. Turning each one up or down mixes every color you have ever seen on a screen. This is additive color, because the lights add together: all three at full strength make white, all three off make black, and red plus green makes yellow. That last one surprises anyone who learned to mix paint, which works the opposite way (subtractive color: each ink absorbs some light). Printers use the subtractive CMYK model (cyan, magenta, yellow and black ink), which is why a bright screen color can come out duller on paper. Screens, and therefore the web, use RGB.

    Each of the three channels is normally stored in one byte, which is 8 bits, so it holds a whole number from 0 (that light off) to 255 (full strength): 256 levels. Three channels of 256 levels give 256 × 256 × 256 = 16,777,216 possible colors per pixel, more than the human eye can tell apart. In CSS you write the three numbers directly:

    Color depth: how many bits per pixel?

    The number of bits each pixel uses is the image's color depth (or bit depth). Every extra bit doubles the number of colors a pixel can hold, and makes the file bigger. Use too few bits and the image runs out of colors: smooth gradients such as a sunset sky break into visible stripes, an effect called banding.

    Images with 8 bits per pixel or fewer usually use a palette (also called indexed color). The file stores a short list of chosen colors once, and each pixel stores only its position in that list, like a paint-by-numbers kit. The table works out the uncompressed size of the same 800 × 600 image at each common depth.

    What it is. Each pixel is a single bit, a 0 or a 1, so the whole image has exactly two colors, usually black and white. Eight pixels fit into one byte.

    When to use it. When only "ink or no ink" matters: a scanned signature, a fax-style document, a simple line drawing, a QR code or a barcode. You rarely pick 1-bit by hand on the web, but PNG and GIF encoders store two-color images extremely compactly.

    Watch out. There is no gray at all, so there is no anti-aliasing either: every curve and every letter edge becomes a hard staircase. A photo turns into black blobs unless it is dithered, scattered with dots that fake shades of gray from a distance.

    What it is. The file holds a palette of up to 256 colors, each one a full 24-bit color. Every pixel then stores a single byte: the number of its color in the palette. A pixel holding 17 means "use palette color number 17".

    When to use it. For graphics with a limited set of flat colors: logos, simple illustrations, diagrams, charts and small animations. GIF is always 8-bit, and PNG can be ("PNG-8"). A good encoder builds an adaptive palette, the 256 colors that matter most in this particular image.

    Watch out. A photo contains tens of thousands of different colors. Squeezing them into 256 causes banding in skies and blotchy skin. Dithering hides the bands with speckles, but the speckles make the file compress worse. GIF also allows only one fully transparent color, so edges cannot fade softly.

    What it is. Each pixel stores its own red, green and blue values, one byte each: 3 bytes per pixel and 16,777,216 possible colors. It is called true color because that is more colors than the eye can distinguish, so gradients look perfectly smooth.

    When to use it. For photographs and anything with gradients, shadows or many colors: product shots, portraits, scenery. JPEG is always 24-bit; PNG-24 and WebP can be.

    Watch out. Uncompressed, it is huge: three bytes for every pixel. A 12-megapixel phone photo is 36,000,000 bytes of raw color. That is why photos are always saved compressed (6.3), and why you resize a photo to the size the page needs before you upload it (6.2).

    What it is. 24-bit color plus a fourth byte per pixel called the alpha channel: how opaque the pixel is, from 0 (fully transparent) to 255 (solid). Values in between let an edge fade smoothly into whatever is behind it.

    When to use it. For logos, icons and cut-out product shots that sit on top of different backgrounds, and for soft shadows and glows. PNG-32, WebP and AVIF support alpha.

    Watch out. It is a third bigger than 24-bit for the same pixels, and the extra byte is wasted when every pixel is solid: a photo with a plain background saved as PNG-32 is a huge file for no benefit. JPEG has no alpha channel at all, so transparent areas turn white or black when you convert to it.

    Which color depth should I pick?
    • A photo, or anything with gradients: 24-bit. Example: save the beach photo as a JPEG or WebP.
    • A logo, icon or diagram with a few flat colors: 8-bit palette (PNG-8 or GIF), or better still a vector (SVG).
    • Anything with transparent or soft edges that sits on different backgrounds: 32-bit. Example: a cut-out product shot as PNG-32 or WebP.
    • Pure black-and-white line art or a QR code: 1-bit is enough; any PNG encoder will store it compactly.

    How big is an image, really?

    Put the pieces together and you can work out the size of any raster image before compression:

    uncompressed bytes = width × height × bits per pixel ÷ 8

    A full-HD 1920 × 1080 photo at 24 bits is 1920 × 1080 × 24 ÷ 8 = 6,220,800 bytes, about 5.9 MB. The file you download is much smaller because it is compressed (6.3), but once the browser decodes it to put it on screen, it needs all of those bytes again in memory (in fact a little more, because browsers store 4 bytes per pixel). Large dimensions cost memory no matter how small the file is.

    Raster versus vector

    A raster image has a fixed number of pixels. Show it larger than that and the browser has to invent the missing pixels: it either smooths between neighbors, which looks blurry, or repeats each pixel as a bigger block, which looks jagged. Either way, no new detail appears. A vector image has no pixels to run out of: the browser redraws its shapes from the instructions at whatever size you ask for, so edges stay crisp at 16 pixels or on a billboard.

    Image Concepts

    An image file has a fixed number of pixels, but a page can show it at any size, on a cheap laptop or a phone with three times the pixel density. Getting from one to the other well means the image looks sharp, downloads fast, keeps its proportions and doesn't make the page jump while it loads. This section gives you the ideas and the HTML to do all four.

    Dimensions versus display size

    The dimensions of an image are the pixels in the file: its intrinsic (or natural) size. The display size is how big the page shows it, measured in CSS pixels and set by HTML attributes or CSS. They do not have to match, and when they don't, the browser scales the picture:

    • Displayed smaller than its dimensions: it looks fine, but every visitor downloads pixels nobody sees. A 4000-pixel-wide photo shown 400 pixels wide sends 100 times the pixels needed (10 times the width × 10 times the height).
    • Displayed larger than its dimensions: the browser has to stretch the pixels it has, and the picture goes soft.

    Resolution: ppi, dpi and device pixels

    Resolution is how tightly pixels are packed. Screens and image files measure it in ppi (pixels per inch); printers measure it in dpi (dots of ink per inch). It matters a lot on paper: a sharp print needs about 300 dpi, so a 6 × 4 inch photo print needs 1800 × 1200 pixels.

    On the web, the ppi number stored inside a file is ignored. An image "saved at 300 ppi" is no sharper on a page than the same pixels "saved at 72 ppi"; it only carries a different label. Browsers care about one thing: the pixel dimensions.

    What does change sharpness is the screen. CSS measures lengths in CSS pixels (about 1/96 of an inch at arm's length, as you saw in 4.2). Older screens draw each CSS pixel with exactly one physical dot, a device pixel. Modern phones and many laptops pack 2 or 3 device pixels into each CSS pixel in each direction, so 4 or 9 dots where there used to be one. That number is the device pixel ratio (DPR), and JavaScript can read it as window.devicePixelRatio. An image displayed 400 CSS pixels wide covers 800 device pixels on a 2× screen, so to use every one of those dots it needs a file 800 pixels wide.

      Resampling: shrinking and enlarging

      Changing an image's pixel dimensions is called resampling. The two directions are not equal:

      • Downsampling (making it smaller) combines groups of pixels into fewer pixels. Detail is lost, but what remains looks clean. This is what you should do to every photo before you put it on a page.
      • Upsampling (making it bigger) has to invent pixels that were never captured. Copying the nearest pixel (nearest neighbor) looks blocky; blending neighbors (bilinear or bicubic) looks blurry. Neither brings back lost detail: "zoom and enhance" only works on television.

      So keep your original, full-size file safe and export each smaller copy from it. Shrinking a copy and then enlarging it again is a one-way trip. Try it:

        Aspect ratio

        The aspect ratio is the shape of an image: its width compared to its height, written width : height and reduced like a fraction. A 1920 × 1080 photo is 16 : 9, because both numbers divide by 120. The common ones are worth knowing: 16 : 9 (video and widescreen), 4 : 3 (older screens, many tablets), 3 : 2 (most cameras), 1 : 1 (square social posts) and 9 : 16 (phone stories).

        Whenever you resize, keep the ratio. Change the width and the height by different amounts and the picture is stretched or squashed: faces get wider, the sun becomes an oval. To work out a new size by hand, use new height = new width × old height ÷ old width. Resizing that 1920 × 1080 photo to 800 wide gives 800 × 1080 ÷ 1920 = 450, so 800 × 450. In CSS you let the browser do this math: set one dimension and leave the other auto.

        Sometimes the box on the page has a different shape from the photo, such as a square thumbnail for a wide picture. The CSS object-fit property decides what happens: fill (the default) stretches the image to the box's shape, contain fits the whole image inside and leaves empty bars, and cover fills the box completely and crops whatever spills over.

          Responsive images: srcset, sizes and picture

          Your visitors use 360-pixel phones and 2560-pixel monitors, at device pixel ratios of 1, 2 or 3. No single file suits them all: a file big enough for the monitor wastes a phone's data and time, and a file small enough for the phone looks blurry on the monitor. Responsive images solve this. You list several versions of the image in the HTML, describe how big it will be displayed, and the browser picks the best one before it downloads anything.           What it is. Two attributes giving the image's dimensions in pixels, without units: width="1600" height="1000". They are not a promise about the display size. The browser divides one by the other to learn the image's shape before a single byte arrives, so with height: auto in your CSS it reserves an empty box of exactly the right height straight away.

          When to use it. On every <img> (and every <video>). Use the file's real dimensions; with srcset, any one file's dimensions work, because all the versions share one shape.

          Watch out. Pair them with max-width: 100% and height: auto in CSS. Without height: auto, the height attribute wins and the photo is squashed when a narrow screen shrinks its width. Leave the attributes off and text jumps down when the image arrives, an effect called layout shift that you can watch happen in the demo further down.

          What it is. The image's text alternative, which you met in 3.5. Screen readers read it aloud, browsers show it when the file fails to load, and search engines use it to understand the picture.

          When to use it. On every <img>. Describe what the image communicates in this context, in a sentence or less. For a purely decorative image, keep the attribute but leave it empty, alt="", and screen readers skip it.

          Watch out. Don't start with "Image of": screen readers already announce it as an image. Don't stuff it with keywords, and don't repeat a caption word for word. For an image that is a link, describe where the link goes ("Home"), not the picture. Leaving alt out entirely is worse than empty: many screen readers then read out the file name.

          What it is. A list of files for different pixel densities, each followed by an x descriptor: srcset="logo-200.png 1x, logo-400.png 2x". The browser compares the descriptors with its own device pixel ratio and downloads just one file.

          When to use it. For images shown at the same CSS size on every screen: logos, avatars, icons, small thumbnails. A logo displayed at 200 × 60 needs a 200 × 60 file for 1× screens and a 400 × 120 file for 2× and 3× screens.

          Watch out. x only describes density. If the image changes width with the layout (full width on a phone, half width on a laptop), use w descriptors and sizes instead. Keep a plain src as the fallback, and remember a 3× file is nine times the pixels of a 1× file: usually only worth it for small, crisp graphics.

          What it is. srcset lists the files with their real pixel widths (480w means the file is 480 pixels wide), and sizes tells the browser how wide the image will be displayed. The browser needs sizes because it chooses the file before it has loaded your CSS. It then works out display width × device pixel ratio and picks the smallest file that is at least that wide.

          When to use it. For images that stretch with the layout: hero banners, photos in articles, gallery and product images.

          Watch out. Leave out sizes and the browser assumes 100vw, the full window width, and may download a much bigger file than needed. Keep sizes roughly in line with your CSS. The number before w is the file's width in pixels, not a CSS width. Never mix x and w descriptors in one srcset.

          What it is. A <picture> element wraps one <img> and any number of <source> elements. The browser uses the first <source> whose media query matches the window and falls back to the <img>. The <img> is always required: it is the element actually displayed, and it carries the alt, width and height.

          When to use it. For art direction, when a small screen needs a different crop, not just a smaller file. A wide harbor panorama works on a laptop, but on a phone the kayaks would be specks, so you serve a tight square close-up instead.

          Watch out. The first match wins, so put the most specific condition first. Style the <img>, not the <picture>. And don't use <picture> just to serve smaller files of the same crop: srcset with sizes does that more simply and lets the browser choose.

          What it is. The same <picture> structure, but each <source> names its file format in type. The browser skips formats it cannot decode and uses the first one it can, without downloading the others.

          When to use it. To send newer, smaller formats such as AVIF and WebP (6.3) to browsers that understand them, with a JPEG that works everywhere as the safety net.

          Watch out. List the newest, smallest format first. type must be the exact MIME type (image/avif, image/webp). The <img> fallback must be a format every browser supports, such as JPEG or PNG.

          What it is. An attribute that tells the browser not to download the image until the visitor scrolls close to it.

          When to use it. For images below the first screenful: photos further down a long article, a gallery, a grid of products, avatars in a comment thread. Visitors who never scroll that far never download them.

          Watch out. Never lazy-load the main image at the top of the page; that only delays the thing everyone sees first. For that one, fetchpriority="high" asks the browser to fetch it early. Lazy images still need width and height, or the page jumps when they finally arrive.

          Which one should I pick?
          • Every image: alt, width and height, plus img { max-width: 100%; height: auto; } in your CSS.
          • An image that is always the same size (logo, avatar): srcset with 1x and 2x.
          • An image that changes width with the layout (photos, banners): srcset with w descriptors, plus sizes.
          • A different crop on small screens: <picture> with <source media>.
          • Modern formats with a safety net: <picture> with <source type>.
          • Below the first screenful: loading="lazy". At the top: never lazy.

            File Formats

            Raw pixel data is enormous: you worked out in 6.1 that one full-HD photo is about 6 MB of color values. Every image format is a different way of squeezing those numbers into fewer bytes. Formats differ in how they squeeze, how many colors they allow, whether they support transparency and animation, and which browsers can open them. Picking the right one is often the single biggest speed-up you can give a page.

            Lossless versus lossy compression

            Compression means storing the same picture in fewer bytes. There are two families:

            • Lossless compression gives back every pixel exactly when the file is opened. It works by spotting repetition: "200 white pixels in a row" takes far less space than writing white 200 times. PNG, GIF and lossless WebP work this way. It is excellent for flat graphics, text and screenshots, and poor for photos, whose natural grain means almost nothing repeats exactly.
            • Lossy compression throws away detail your eye is unlikely to miss, such as tiny color variations and fine texture, then compresses what is left. You choose a quality level: lower quality means a smaller file and more artifacts (blocky squares, halos around edges, smeared texture). JPEG, lossy WebP and AVIF work this way. It is excellent for photos and poor for text and sharp edges.

            One rule follows: every time you open and re-save a lossy file, a little more detail is lost (generation loss). Keep your original, edit that, and export the lossy copy once.

            Run-length encoding: lossless compression in action

            The simplest lossless trick is run-length encoding (RLE): replace each run of identical pixels with a count and a color. The 16-pixel row WWWWWWBBBBWWWWWW becomes 6W 4B 6W, just three runs. GIF and PNG use cleverer relatives (LZW and DEFLATE) that also find repeated patterns, but the principle is the same: repetition compresses, randomness does not. Type or paint a row, then step through the encoder one run at a time.

                Every format at this quality

                  What did the JPEG encoder actually do to your picture? Step through it. Only one step throws information away, and the quality slider controls exactly that step.

                  1Brightness and color apart
                  2Color at lower resolution
                  38 × 8 blocks
                  4Blocks as patterns
                  5Round off detail (lossy)
                  6Pack into a .jpg

                  1. The encoder separates brightness from color. Each pixel's red, green and blue become one brightness value (luma) and two color values (chroma). Your eyes notice fine detail in brightness far more than fine detail in color, and the next steps exploit that.

                  2. Chroma subsampling: the two color channels are usually stored at half the width and half the height, a quarter of the data, while brightness keeps every pixel. Most people never notice; thin red text on a blue background is where it shows.

                  3. The picture is cut into blocks of 8 × 8 pixels, and each block is handled on its own. That is why heavy JPEG compression shows up as a grid of little squares, as you saw at low quality in the lab.

                  4. Each block is rewritten as a mix of 64 standard patterns, from one flat color up to the finest checkerboard (a calculation called the discrete cosine transform). A smooth patch of sky needs only a few of them; an edge or a texture needs many.

                  5. Quantization, the lossy step: the amount of each pattern is divided by a number and rounded to a whole number. The quality setting chooses those numbers. At low quality, most of the fine-detail patterns round down to zero, and that detail is gone for good.

                  6. Finally the numbers, including long runs of zeros, are packed losslessly (with run-length and Huffman coding, relatives of the RLE above) into the .jpg file. Opening the file undoes every step except the rounding in step 5.

                  What it is. The Joint Photographic Experts Group format, from 1992, and still the most common photo format on the web. It cuts the image into 8 × 8 pixel blocks, keeps the broad color changes in each block and discards fine detail according to the quality setting. Always 24-bit, never transparent.

                  When to use it. For photographs and realistic images with smooth gradients: product photos, portraits, scenery, big banner photos. Quality 70 to 85 is the usual sweet spot, where the file is small and the artifacts are hard to see.

                  Watch out. Text, line art, logos and screenshots get fuzzy halos and blocky squares around every sharp edge (try the flat graphic at low quality in the lab). There is no transparency: transparent areas become white or black. And every re-save loses a little more.

                  What it is. Portable Network Graphics, from 1996, created as a patent-free replacement for GIF. Lossless. It comes in three flavors: PNG-8 (a palette of up to 256 colors), PNG-24 (true color) and PNG-32 (true color plus an alpha channel, so edges and shadows can be partly transparent).

                  When to use it. For screenshots, diagrams, charts and logos, and anything with text or hard edges that must stay crisp. Also for any image that needs soft transparency, such as a cut-out product photo with a shadow.

                  Watch out. A photo saved as PNG is huge, often five to ten times the size of a good JPEG, because photo grain never repeats exactly. Use PNG-8 when the image has few colors, and run every PNG through an optimizer before publishing; it can often shrink the file by a third with no visible change.

                  What it is. The Graphics Interchange Format, from 1987. Lossless compression, but limited to a palette of 256 colors per frame, and only one palette color can be transparent, and only fully. Its claim to fame is animation: a GIF can hold a series of frames that play in a loop.

                  When to use it. For small, simple animations that must play everywhere, such as a loading spinner in an email newsletter (many email programs animate GIFs but not video).

                  Watch out. An animated GIF made from real video is enormous: a five-second clip can weigh 5 to 10 MB, while the same clip as an MP4 or WebM video (6.4) is a few hundred kilobytes. On web pages, use a muted, looping video instead. GIF's all-or-nothing transparency also leaves jagged, light-colored fringes on dark backgrounds.

                  What it is. Scalable Vector Graphics: a text format (written in XML, a cousin of HTML) that describes shapes, paths, text and gradients. You can use an SVG file as an image source, or paste the SVG code straight into your HTML, where CSS can style it.

                  When to use it. For logos, icons, illustrations, charts and maps: anything made of shapes. One file stays crisp at every size and every pixel density, so you never need separate 2× versions.

                  Watch out. Not for photos: describing every pixel as a shape makes a gigantic file. SVGs exported from design tools carry a lot of extra metadata, so optimize them. And never put SVG files uploaded by strangers on your site without cleaning them first: SVG can contain scripts.

                  What it is. A format from Google, from 2010, that can be lossy or lossless and supports alpha transparency and animation. A lossy WebP is typically 25 to 35% smaller than a JPEG of similar quality, and a lossless WebP is usually smaller than the same PNG.

                  When to use it. As an everyday format for photos and graphics on modern sites, as a smaller replacement for transparent PNG-32s, and for short animations instead of GIF. Every current major browser displays it.

                  Watch out. Very old browsers cannot show it, so if you must support them, use <picture> with a JPEG or PNG fallback. Some desktop programs and email clients still can't open a .webp file that a visitor downloads.

                  What it is. The AV1 Image File Format, from 2019, built on the AV1 video codec. Lossy or lossless, with alpha, animation, and wide color and HDR (up to 12 bits per channel). It usually makes the smallest files of all, often around half the size of a comparable JPEG.

                  When to use it. For photos on pages where speed matters most, listed first in a <picture> element so other browsers fall back gracefully.

                  Watch out. Encoding is slow, and not every tool can create AVIF yet; your browser may be able to display AVIF while JavaScript cannot create it (the lab tells you). Always provide a fallback. At very low quality it smears fine texture, so skin and fabric can look plastic.

                  What it is. The little icon on browser tabs, bookmarks and phone home screens is the favicon. Its classic format is ICO, a container that holds several small images (16 × 16, 32 × 32, 48 × 48) in one file so the browser can pick the size it needs. Modern browsers also accept PNG and SVG favicons.

                  When to use it. On every site. Put favicon.ico in the site's root folder (browsers ask for it automatically), add an SVG icon for modern browsers, and a 180 × 180 PNG apple-touch-icon for iPhone home screens.

                  Watch out. At 16 × 16 pixels, detail disappears: design a simplified mark, such as one letter or symbol, not a shrunken logo. The <link> elements belong in the <head>. Browsers cache favicons stubbornly, so a new icon may take a while to appear.

                  Which format should I pick?
                  • A photo: JPEG at quality 75 to 85, or AVIF and WebP with a JPEG fallback in <picture>.
                  • A logo, icon, chart or illustration: SVG.
                  • A screenshot, a graphic with text, or anything with soft transparency: PNG (or lossless WebP).
                  • A short looping animation: a muted, looping MP4 or WebM video; GIF only when it must play everywhere, as in email.
                  • The site icon: favicon.ico, plus an SVG icon and an apple-touch-icon PNG.

                  Audio and Video

                  Until HTML5 arrived around 2010, playing sound or video on a web page needed a browser plug-in such as Adobe Flash. Today two elements, <audio> and <video>, play media natively, with built-in controls that work with a keyboard, captions, and no plug-in at all. Every sound and video in this section is generated by JavaScript inside your browser, so nothing is downloaded.

                  Containers and codecs

                  A media file has two layers, and the browser must understand both before it can play anything:

                  • The container is the file format: a box that holds one or more streams (video, audio, sometimes captions) together with timing information. MP4, WebM and Ogg are containers, and the file extension usually names the container.
                  • A codec (short for coder-decoder) is the compression method used inside the box for each stream. H.264, VP9 and AV1 compress video; AAC, MP3 and Opus compress audio.

                  Think of the container as a lunch box and the codecs as the recipes for what is inside. Two files that both end in .mp4 can hold different codecs, which is why a browser may play one and refuse the other. Like photos, media is almost always compressed lossily, because raw media is huge: CD-quality audio is 44,100 samples per second × 2 bytes × 2 channels, about 10 MB per minute, while a 128 kbps MP3 of the same minute is about 0.9 MB.

                  What it is. MP4 (MPEG-4 Part 14) is the most widely supported media container. The video inside is almost always H.264 (also called AVC) and the audio AAC. Phones and cameras record this combination by default.

                  When to use it. As your safe default for video: every browser and device plays H.264 in MP4. List it last among the sources as the fallback, or use it alone if you only make one file.

                  Watch out. H.264 is an older codec, so its files are bigger than VP9 or AV1 at the same quality. "MP4" only names the box: an MP4 holding HEVC (H.265) video, which some phones record, won't play in many browsers. Export with the "web optimized" (fast start) option so playback can begin before the whole file has arrived.

                  What it is. An open, royalty-free container from Google (2010). It holds VP9 or AV1 video (VP8 in older files) and Opus or Vorbis audio.

                  When to use it. As the first source, to save bandwidth: VP9 and especially AV1 deliver the same quality as H.264 in noticeably fewer bytes. Large video sites stream VP9 and AV1 to every browser that can play them.

                  Watch out. Older Apple devices have patchy support, so always follow it with an MP4 fallback. AV1 is slow to encode and needs a fairly recent device to decode smoothly.

                  What it is. An older open container from the Xiph.Org Foundation. Audio files (.ogg, .oga) hold Vorbis or Opus; video files (.ogv) hold Theora, a video codec that is now obsolete.

                  When to use it. Mostly for audio today: an Ogg Opus or Ogg Vorbis file is an open, compact alternative to MP3, popular for game sound effects.

                  Watch out. Don't use Theora video at all; use WebM instead. Some older Apple devices can't play Ogg, so give audio an MP3 fallback.

                  What it is. MPEG-1 Audio Layer III, from 1993: the format that made digital music portable. It is both a codec and a file format (.mp3). It is lossy: it drops sounds that are masked by louder ones or are hard to hear. Quality is set by the bitrate in kilobits per second: 128 kbps is good, 192 to 320 kbps very good.

                  When to use it. For music, podcasts and any sound you want to play absolutely everywhere, including after a visitor downloads it and opens it in some other app.

                  Watch out. It is older and less efficient than AAC or Opus, and below about 96 kbps it starts to sound swishy and metallic. Its MIME type is audio/mpeg, not audio/mp3.

                  What it is. Advanced Audio Coding, the successor to MP3 (1997). At the same bitrate it sounds better than MP3. It is normally stored in an MP4 container, as the soundtrack of a video or as an audio-only .m4a file.

                  When to use it. As the audio track of MP4 videos, and for audio-only files such as interviews and podcast episodes.

                  Watch out. Because it lives in an MP4 box, its type is audio/mp4. The codec string mp4a.40.2 means AAC-LC, the common everyday profile.

                  What it is. A modern, open, royalty-free audio codec (2012) that handles speech and music equally well with very little delay. It is the codec behind voice and video calls in the browser. It is stored in WebM or Ogg.

                  When to use it. For speech, podcasts, voice messages and game sounds, and as the soundtrack of WebM videos. 32 to 64 kbps is plenty for speech.

                  Watch out. Give it an MP3 or AAC fallback for very old Apple devices.

                  Which formats should I offer?
                  • Video: WebM (VP9 or AV1) first, then MP4 (H.264 + AAC) as the fallback. Only making one file? MP4 with H.264.
                  • Audio: MP3 alone plays everywhere. Add an Opus file first if you want smaller downloads.
                  • A short silent loop that replaces a GIF: MP4 or WebM with autoplay muted loop playsinline.
                  • Uncompressed WAV: only for tiny sound effects, or as the master file you encode the others from.

                    Accessible media

                    Sound and moving pictures shut people out unless you provide alternatives. Plan for all of these:

                    • Captions: every spoken word plus meaningful sounds ([door slams], [upbeat music]) as text on the video, for people who are deaf or hard of hearing, and for the many people watching with the sound off.
                    • Subtitles: a translation of the dialogue, for people who can hear it but don't understand the language.
                    • A transcript: the whole text on the page, next to the player. It helps everyone who prefers reading, works for podcasts (which have no picture to caption), and can be searched and indexed.
                    • Audio description: narration of important visual information (what is shown, who is doing what) for people who are blind, either as a described version of the video or a descriptions track.
                    • Control: use controls (or fully keyboard-accessible custom controls), never autoplay sound, and let people pause anything that moves.

                      Working with Color

                      Color sets the mood of a page, makes a brand recognizable and tells visitors where to look. It also decides whether they can read your page at all. In 4.2 you learned to write colors in CSS. This section goes further: how the notations relate to the pixels from 6.1, how to pick colors that work together, how to measure readability with real numbers, and how to design for the one man in twelve who sees color differently.

                      Color notations, side by side

                      Every CSS color notation describes the same thing: how much red, green and blue light a pixel should give off, plus optionally how opaque it is. Hex writes each channel as two hexadecimal digits (base 16, where a to f stand for 10 to 15), so each pair runs from 00 (0) to ff (15 × 16 + 15 = 255). rgb() writes the same three numbers in ordinary decimal. hsl() describes the color the way people think about it: hue (an angle on the color wheel: 0 red, 120 green, 240 blue), saturation (0% gray to 100% vivid) and lightness (0% black, 50% the pure color, 100% white).

                      What it is. A # followed by three pairs of hexadecimal digits, one pair per channel: red, green, blue. To read a pair, multiply the first digit by 16 and add the second: c0 is 12 × 16 + 0 = 192.

                      When to use it. When you copy colors from a design tool, a brand guide or a color picker. It is the most common notation in real stylesheets because it is short and exact.

                      Watch out. Hex is hard to adjust by eye: which digits would you change to make #2e73b8 slightly lighter? Without the # the value is invalid and silently ignored. The 3-digit short form only works when each pair has two equal digits: #ff8800 shortens to #f80, but #2e73b8 has no short form.

                      What it is. The same three channel values in decimal, from 0 to 255. The modern syntax separates them with spaces, rgb(46 115 184); the older comma syntax, rgb(46, 115, 184), still works everywhere.

                      When to use it. When you think in amounts of light, or when JavaScript calculates colors. The magnifier in 6.1 showed every pixel in exactly this form.

                      Watch out. Don't mix commas and spaces in one value: it becomes invalid. Making a color lighter or darker means changing all three numbers in the right proportion, which is guesswork; hsl() does it with one number.

                      What it is. Hue, saturation and lightness. The hue is an angle on the color wheel: 0 red, 60 yellow, 120 green, 180 cyan, 240 blue, 300 magenta, and back to red at 360. Saturation runs from 0% (gray) to 100% (vivid). Lightness runs from 0% (black) through 50% (the pure color) to 100% (white).

                      When to use it. To build a family of related colors: keep the hue and saturation and change only the lightness for a hover state, a border or a background tint. Color schemes are angles on the wheel, so with hsl() they become simple arithmetic, as the scheme generator below shows.

                      Watch out. The same lightness does not look equally bright in every hue: hsl(60 100% 50%) (yellow) is far brighter to the eye than hsl(240 100% 50%) (blue). Judge readability by the contrast ratio, never by the L number.

                      What it is. A fourth value giving the opacity, from 0 (invisible) to 1 or 100% (solid). In rgb() and hsl() it goes after a slash: rgb(0 0 0 / 50%). Older code writes rgba(0, 0, 0, 0.5), which means the same. Hex adds a fourth pair: #00000080.

                      When to use it. For a dark layer behind text on a photo, shadows, subtle tints that work on any background, and focus rings.

                      Watch out. A see-through color looks different depending on what is behind it, so check contrast against the real mix you end up with. And alpha is not the same as the opacity property, which fades the whole element, its text and children included.

                      What they are. 148 keywords such as red, tomato, navy and rebeccapurple, plus two special ones: transparent, and currentColor, which means "this element's own text color".

                      When to use them. For quick experiments and prototypes. currentColor is genuinely useful in finished code: a border or an icon that always matches the text.

                      Watch out. A brand's colors almost never match a name exactly. The names are inconsistent too: darkgray is lighter than gray. And plain gray text on white is only 3.95 : 1, which fails for body text.

                      • A border or icon that matches the text: currentColor.

                        Color psychology and branding

                        Colors come with associations, which is why brands choose theirs so carefully. In many Western countries, blue suggests trust and calm (banks, insurers, social networks), red energy and urgency (sales, fast food), green nature, health and money, yellow and orange friendliness and optimism, purple luxury and creativity, and black elegance. These meanings are cultural, not universal: white is worn at weddings in much of the West and at funerals in parts of East Asia, and red means luck and celebration in China but danger on a warning sign.

                        So choose colors with your audience in mind, use them the same way on every page so they become recognizable, and remember that no brand color is worth unreadable text. That is what the rest of this section is about.

                        Contrast: can everyone read it?

                        The contrast ratio compares how bright two colors look, from 1 : 1 (identical) to 21 : 1 (black on white). It is calculated from each color's relative luminance: the channels are converted to amounts of light and weighted by how sensitive the eye is to each, 0.2126 × red + 0.7152 × green + 0.0722 × blue. Green counts most, which is why yellow text on white (1.07 : 1) is nearly invisible. Then ratio = (lighter + 0.05) ÷ (darker + 0.05).                           Designing for color blindness

                          About 1 man in 12 and 1 woman in 200 have a color vision deficiency (CVD). The most common forms affect red and green: protanopia (no working red cones) and deuteranopia (no working green cones), plus their milder "-anomaly" versions. Tritanopia (blue and yellow) is rare, and achromatopsia (no color at all) rarer still. Add the people reading your page on a dim phone in sunlight, or printing it in grayscale, and the rule is clear: never use color as the only way to show information. Pair it with text, icons, patterns or position. The simulator filters a small dashboard through each type of vision; switch on the fix to see how little it takes.
